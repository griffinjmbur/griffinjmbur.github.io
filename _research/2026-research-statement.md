# My research

My research re-opens the debate on U.S. income inequality. Despite the overall
salience of inequality in academic research and popular discourse, many open
questions remain about its extent and nature. My dissertation tackles three
closely-related questions.

First, I intervene in the long-standing debate on the determinants of
inequality. Using the coefficient of variation decomposition, I demonstrate that
the majority contributor to inequality of incomes is capital income &mdash;
essentially, income from wealth &mdash; rather than wage income, the focus of
the great majority of sociological research. I show that this surprising result
holds only if high-quality data sources, such as tax data or the Survey of
Consumer Finances, are used, a fact obscured from the sociolocial vision in part
thanks to the common use of survey data such as the Current Population Survey or
General Social Survey, which only capture a truncated view of the extent and
sources of income of top earners.

This is no mere technical finding: most sociological research on the topic
trains its explanatory lens on labor-market mechanisms (e.g. education, skills,
exposure to offshoring), leaving it unable to convincingly explain this
largely-unkown empirical result: wage income is far from the whole story. I
suggest that an important theoretical consequence follows: sociologists should
broaden our focus beyond those labor-market mechanisms and (re-)incorporate the
role of ownership relations into the analysis. While a small stream of existing
research has taken such a "property relations" approach, it has done so by
constructing static matrices of social classes, and it has tended to become
mired in debate: "which axes should generate our class map?" is a very difficult
question. I put forward a convincing alternative &mdash; instead of viewing
individuals as members of fixed "classes" defined by a crude owner-employee
binary, we might instead think of individuals as combining the economic
functions of both workers and capitalists, even if very many persons largely
spend their lives in only one of those roles. This parsimonious
conceptualization of "class" as a spectrum, defined by the extent of asset
ownership, allows us to avoid the murky waters of class schemes while preserving
the critical insight that the unequal distribution of wealth dominates the role
of the unequal distribution of labor-market characteristics in the empirical
analysis.

The remaining two chapters further investigate this puzzle, broadly construed:
why can we say so little with confidence about the nature of inequality? In my
second chapter, I suggest that the definition of "income" itself is at the heart
of this lacuna. Though contested among economists, "income" as a theoretical
object is rarely addressed by sociologists. In fact, some economists suggest
that expanding the definition of income to include capital income as discussed
above, but also fringe benefits (such as employer-sponsored health insurance) or
even "imputed rent" (the hypothetical rental value of one's home), significantly
*decreases* measured trends in inequality, issuing a challenge to the consensus
that inequality in the United States has risen sharply in recent decades.

My second chapter addresses this challenge in two ways. First, I systematically
demonstrate the marginal effects of various common ways of defining income on
inequality. My research shows that, relative to the use of wages *alone*,
expanding the definition of income in fact almost invariably produces an
*increased* trend in inequality (over the years 1980 to 2020), in stark contrast
to previous research. The paper goes on to make a theoretical contribution: I
demonstrate that many of the circulating definitions of income are theoretically
ambiguous. For instance, it is not clear whether to include health care benefits
or forgone rent in measures of income inequality. In this chapter, I review the
benefits and limitations of different measures of income, and suggest a simple,
conceptually defensible measure for general use by sociologists.

Finally, in my third chapter, I move from the measurement of income to the
measurement of inequality itself. Social scientists have taken a somewhat *ad
hoc* approach to the definition and operationalization of inequality. Many
measures in common use, such as the Gini coefficient, have significant
limitations: for example, we cannot easily decompose such measures into
identifiable contributions between and within social groups. I synthesize a
scattered literature that identifies the inequality measures which satisfy
axioms meaningful to social scientists in common-sense terms: that they be
decomposable into sub-groups and factors, independent of scale or population
size, and sensitive to redistribution of incomes. I follow by outlining a novel
method for combining factor and sub-group decompositions which avoids the
problem of path-dependence common in many existing approaches. I also
demonstrate, however, that inequality research is inevitably contentious: even
among the small class of measures which satisfy the criteria outlined above,
one's choice of an inequality measure depends on one's aversion to inequality. I
demonstrate that even small tweaks in this inequality aversion parameter can
make significant changes in our assessment of *which* factors are responsible
for inequality.

In my future research, I plan to expand on the third chapter of my dissertation.
The most interesting finding from this chapter is known to specialists, but it
remains surprising and in need of further research: questions such as "what
causes inequality" depend *strongly* on the measure of inequality selected *and*
the question "what does it mean mathematically for $X$ to cause $Y$?" I am
particularly interested in developing systematically the link between the
unified framework for decomposition analysis based on the Shapley approach, with
which my third paper deals, and recent research by scholars working in the
potential outcomes framework. Researchers in the latter tradition have developed
a robust framework for identifying causal effects in the (ubiquitous) situation
where assignment to a treatment is non-random, while scholars in the former
tradition have focused their attention on the problem of assigning to a given
factor a particular "contribution" to inequality, depending on the inequality
measure and model used. These traditions have developed largely in isolation
from one another, but both fundamentally address the puzzle of "when can we say
that $p$ percent of inequality 'owes' to some factor?"

My research focus has shifted over the course of my time in graduate school. I
began my career as a researcher focused on international development, and my
master's thesis investigated the "premature deindustrialization" of Brazil and
Argentina, that is, the decline in the share of industrial output and employment
in the total, relative to the "normal" trajectory of economic development. I
discovered in the course of this project that I was particularly interested in
(and gifted in the use of) statistical methods: in particular, I realized just
how hard it is to get causal questions "right" and how many different angles one
might take on this problem. I also realized that I wanted my work to speak to a
broader community of scholars, especially those working on the United States,
where I am an active participant in public life. While this shift has lengthened
the amount of time I have spent in graduate school, it has also given me a
strong appreciation for a broad array of statistical methods, as well as a
perspective informed by global social thought.

Finally, I should note the connection of my research to my teaching. I am an
especially teaching-focused scholar, and my research is closely tied to my
pedagogy. As a lecturer, I am particularly interested in helping students
understand statistical methods, and methodology more generally, from the ground
up: I have been successful in helping students come to see *why* certain
fundamentals are true and why so much debate exists among scholars about
essentially everything but those fundamentals. In my own research, I have found
my niche by leaning into this uncertainty.
