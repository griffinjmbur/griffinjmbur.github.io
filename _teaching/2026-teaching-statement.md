# Teaching statement

I am strongly committed to the craft of teaching, and I am particularly
interested in, and proficient at, teaching courses that students often dread:
statistics and computing for social science, and research methods more
generally. I have served as our department's lecturer for its large introductory
statistics course on five occasions; I have also served as the lecturer of the
department's undergraduate research methods course twice and once as lecturer of
its course in data management (along with other experience detailed below).

I am proud to report that I often succeed in engaging even students who expect
to find the material dull; as one student wrote of my introductory statistics
lecture, "*Although I was not interested in the information, the professor made
the class very interesting*". Even if a student feels that she or he is simply
trying to "get through the course", I take pride in helping them do so while
giving them the chance to learn and develop their own skills. A characteristic
comment is one delivered by a student in my advanced statistics laboratory: "*I
felt like I would not have been able to get through this class if it wasn’t for
Griffin*."

This qualitative evidence can be corroborated by quantitative evidence from my
course evaluations: as a lecturer, I achieved a weighted mean across methods and
statistic courses of $4.48$ out of $5$ ($n = 147$). I have also won, or been
nominated by the Sociology Department for, a number of teaching awards. In 2020,
I was the winner of an Excellence in Teaching by a Teaching Assistant award from
the department. I was also the department's Nominee for the 2023–2024 Letters
and Science Teaching Mentors Program as well as the department's Nominee for the
Campus Exceptional Service Award in 2021.

My broadest aim when teaching is to leave students with a healthy, rigorous
skepticism of big claims: most of my students do not go on to conduct scientific
research or need to recall formulae on a daily basis, but as citizens, they do
frequently need to evaluate arguments made in the public sphere. I believe that
learning to reason like a good social scientist serves as an object lesson in
critical thinking more generally &mdash; students may not remember the nuts and
bolts years later, but the experience of successfully mastering those details
once leaves an appreciation for the art of reasoning that is hard to replicate
by other means.

At a more concrete level, I have three fundamental goals for my teaching.

First, I hope for students to come away with mastery of the material &mdash; I
want them to *understand*, rather than *believe*, what social scientists think.
I strive to present students with ideas in their "zone of proximal development":
rather than inundate students with long lists of theoreticians or statisticians
and expect them to struggle to memorize reductive renditions of "who said what",
I outline the basic concepts in sociological research methods and expect
students to grapple with the arguments. For example, in my statistics lectures,
I present brief demonstrations of properties of the variance that are new to
most students but do not go beyond the level of algebra. While it is possible,
and common, to simply *tell* students these properties, I generally do not ask
students to memorize content on my say-so; if there is a complex or novel idea
that I believe that they can understand, I ask them to understand it, and if an
idea is truly too advanced for a given course, I avoid wading in those waters.
In short, I want students to have a clear narrative arc for a course where they
feel ownership of the ideas, and my highest aim is for them to actually enjoy
what can be difficult material. I seek each semester to have every member of the
course feel the way one of my introductory statistics students did: "*[This] is
single-handedly the best math class I've ever taken [...] I was already nervous
taking a course like this because I was never good at math. Yet, the professor
has changed my whole outlook on math*."

Second, I strongly believe in student-oriented, hands-on learning. Even in
lecture courses with more than $100$ students, I commit to memorizing the
majority of my students' names; I offer generous office hours, encourage
students to attend, and generally find that many do. In the lecture hall, I use
this rapport with students to teach in a "call and response" fashion, eliciting
answers from students with relative ease, even when they are not entirely
confident in their understanding yet. I insist that students develop their
comprehension in a multifaceted, practical manner. I expect students to become
comfortable with some pen-and-paper calculations and to be confident using
statistical software. Even in the age of large language models, I hold that it
is important for students to actually play with formulae, to be able to make
mistakes and achieve the satisfaction of struggling and getting a correct
answer, rather than to merely loosely understand the "gist." I have found that
many students in fact thrive when they see that, for example, they can calculate
the correlation coefficient by hand for a small set of data and observe that it
obeys all of the expected properties &mdash; the sign goes the direction they
would hope and the strength is about what they would guess.

Finally, I seek to make the material relevant to students. When I teach
statistics, the course culminates in a research project of their choosing.
Students use real world data (typically the General Social Survey or Current
Population Survey) and work with Stata or Python to answer questions that they
are actually interested in. I provide detailed feedback on project proposals and
the final products, even in large classes (with the help of teaching assistants,
of course). I encourage students to explore controversial questions and to put
their own views to the test. Students report to me sometimes that they update
their priors based on the results, which I think is a strong measure of success:
I know that I have given students the tools to answer questions with so much
confidence that they are willing to change their minds.

Coming to my concrete teaching record, I have served as lecturer for "Statistics
for Sociologists I" on five occasions and the TA once. I have also lectured
"Social Research Methods" (SOC357) twice and "Data Management for Social
Science" (SOC365) once.

Earlier in my career, I served as a TA, focusing on substantial courses related
to my research focus. As a TA, I taught two sections of "Statistics for
Sociologists II" (SOC361) for one semester, three to five sections of "American
Society: How It Really Works" (SOC125) for four semesters, and five sections of
"Population Problems" (SOC170) for two semesters.

In closing, I will mention the courses I am prepared to teach. First, I would be
excited to teach any level of a statistics course aimed at the social sciences,
including graduate courses, and any level of a research methods course. I am
also happy to teach substantive courses on inequality, economic sociology, class
and stratification, introductory demography, and American society writ large. I
am finally very interested in developing new courses: I would be particularly
interested in designing a course introducing advanced students to the literature
on debates concerning inequality.
